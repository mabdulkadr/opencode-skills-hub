# Arabic watch lists

Read this file when the text is Arabic or mixed Arabic and English. The patterns in `SKILL.md` apply to both languages; this file gives the Arabic forms of the watch lists and the rules that only make sense in Arabic.

Apply the same evidence rule: a watch phrase is a reason to look, not an instruction to delete. A phrase used for its technical meaning, inside a quotation, as a title or a proper name, or in a passage that discusses the phrase itself stays as it is.

## A. Staging instead of stating

### 1. Not X but Y

- «ليس مجرد ... بل ...»
- «لا يقتصر على ... وإنما ...»
- «لا يتعلق الأمر بـ ... بل بـ ...»
- «ليس فحسب ... بل ...»
- The same contrast split across two sentences: «هذا لا يعني ...» ثم «هذا يعني ...»
- A clipped negative tail: a closing phrase with no verb, such as «... دون حاجة إلى ...».

Keep the contrast when the negative half corrects a belief the reader holds, or when both halves carry information. Otherwise state the positive half alone.

**Before:**
> ليست هذه مجرد أداة لإدارة المهام، بل هي انعكاس حقيقي لطريقة عمل الفريق بأكمله.
**After:**
> الأداة تعكس طريقة عمل الفريق.

### 2. One-line closers and dramatic fragments

- «وهنا تكمن الأهمية.»
- «وهذا هو الفرق الحقيقي.»
- «ولنا أن ندرك أن ...»
- «المعادلة بسيطة: ...»
- A row of fragments: «لا تردد. لا تأجيل. لا تهاون.»
- «تابعونا للمزيد.» as a closer that adds nothing to a standalone text.

Cut a closer that repeats the paragraph before it. Keep it when it adds a fact or a consequence the reader did not have.

### 3. Sayings that sound deep

- «جوهر المسألة»
- «في حقيقة الأمر»
- «يكمن السر في ...»
- «لغة النجاح» or «... هو عمود ...»
- «... هو جوهر ...»
- «التحول ليس ... بل ...»

Replace the saying with the specific claim the writer can support.

### 4. Staged run-up before the point

- «دعونا نتعمق»
- «لنستكشف»
- «في هذا المقال سنتعرف»
- «إليك كل ما تحتاج إلى معرفته»
- «بدون مقدمات»
- «بصراحة؟»

Remove the run-up and open with the subject, the decision, or the first useful fact.

### 5. Arguing with no one

- «حتى لا يُفهم من ذلك أن ...»
- «لا أقصد القول إن ...»
- «قد يعتقد البعض أن ...»
- «من المهم الإشارة إلى أن ...» when it defends against a view nobody raised
- «من الواضح أن ...» used to pre-empt a disagreement

Remove the defense. Keep an objection the text attributes or answers in full.

## B. Rhythm by rule

### 6. Forced triads

Three adjectives in a row, three parallel clauses, three short sentences that end with a lesson, or a list of three benefits that would read as well with two or five.

Keep three items when the meaning needs three. In Arabic, three is also the natural length of a complete sentence, so watch for triads built from clauses that could merge into one sentence with «و».

### 7. Repeated sentence openings

Consecutive sentences that begin with the same subject, often the same verb repeated three times: «يقول ... ويقول ... ويؤكد ...». Merge them, change the subject, or begin with the action.

### 8. Dashes and long punctuation

Arabic prose rarely uses the em dash as a connector. Treat a cluster of dashes between clauses as the same tell described in section 8 of `SKILL.md`. Ordinary hyphenation inside a word, a number range, or a technical term stays.

### 9. Stacked qualifiers

- «قد يكون من الممكن ربما ...»
- «ربما يحتمل أن ...»
- «من المحتمل في بعض الأحيان أن ...»
- «قد يعد في نظر بعض المراقبين ...»

Keep the degree of uncertainty the evidence supports. Remove the redundant hedge. Keep legal, safety, and scope qualifiers.

### 10. Hyphenated pairs

Drop the hyphen a machine added after the noun, as in «التقرير عالي الجودة». Keep the hyphen the dictionary requires, as in «طرف ثالث» or «متعدد الأطراف». Never change the diacritics of a religious text, a quotation, or a name.

### 11. Passive voice and missing actors

Arabic passive with «قُد» or «تم» hides the actor. Name it when the source names it. A missing subject often shows up as a sentence that starts with «تم ...» and no actor at all.

## C. Inflation and borrowed authority

### 12. Overused AI words

محوري، جوهري، بالغ الأهمية، راسخ، متكامل، استثنائي، نوعي، مستدام، ثري، حيوي، متين، عميق، متشابك، يسلط الضوء على، يعزز، يرسخ، يجسد، يشكل ركيزة، يعكس بوضوح، يبرز، يكمن في جوهر

Keep a word when it is precise and supported. Words such as «عميق» and «متين» are exact in technical writing.

### 13. Inflated significance

- «يمثل نقلة نوعية»
- «يشكل علامة فارقة»
- «يلعب دوراً محورياً»
- «يعد ركيزة أساسية»
- «يترك بصمة لا تمحى»
- «في خضم هذه التحديات، يستمر ...»
- «وفي الختام» when the closing only summarizes
- «تفتح آفاقا واعدة»
- «المستقبل يحمل الكثير من الفرص»
- «خطوة في الاتجاه الصحيح»

Keep the fact and drop the significance. End on the last concrete fact.

### 14. Vague connection

- «مرتبط بـ ...»
- «على صلة بـ ...»
- «في إطار التعاون مع ...»
- «يمثل جزءا من ...»

Name the relationship the source gives: أسس، يعمل في، مول، استحوذ على، تعاون مع، شارك. If the source does not say, keep the vague wording.

### 15. Shallow participial riders

- «مما يعكس»
- «بما يؤكد»
- «الامر الذي يبرز»
- «بما يبرز»
- «وهو ما يسهم في»
- «مشهودا ب»

Keep the rider only when it states a supported consequence.

### 16. Sales language

رائد، استثنائي، مذهل، عريق، فريد من نوعه، وجهة لا تفوت، يقع في قلب، يملك إرثا غنيا، طبيعة ساحرة، صنعة يدوية فريدة

State what the thing is. In advertising copy, keep the persuasion but drop the unsupported superlatives.

### 17. Borrowed authority

- «يرى الخبراء»
- «يشير المختصون»
- «بحسب العديد من التقارير»
- «يحظى باعتراف واسع»
- «أجرت دراسة ...» when no study is named

Name the source and what it said. Otherwise drop the attribution.

### 18. Avoiding simple verbs

- «يمثل» بدل «هو»
- «يحتضن» بدل «يضم»
- «يضطلع بدور» بدل «يقوم بـ»
- «يعد» بدل «هو»
- «يحتل مكانة» بدل «يقع»

Use the simplest accurate verb. Keep the longer verb when it carries a real distinction, as in «يمثل» for a symbol or a role.

## D. Formatting by rule

### 19. Bold as decoration

Bold on every term, every list label, or every phrase without a scanning purpose. Remove the bold. Turn a labeled list into prose when the labels carry no information of their own.

### 20. Decorative headings

- Emojis or arrows on headings and list items.
- A horizontal rule between every section.
- A heading that repeats the document title.
- A heading written for drama rather than to describe the section.

Arabic headings do not need title case. Use the sentence shape the writer uses.

### 21. Quotation marks

Normalize curly quotes only when the target format or the writer's sample uses straight quotes. Do not change the guillemets «» inside Arabic text that uses them as the writer's own convention.

## E. Leftovers from the chat and the draft

### 22. Chatbot residue

بالطبع!، سؤال رائع!، أتمنى أن يكون هذا مفيداً، هل ترغب في المزيد؟، لا تتردد في طلب المساعدة، إليك ملخصا، سأجيب على سؤالك

Remove conversational wrappers from standalone articles and documents. Keep greetings and sign-offs in messages where they belong.

### 23. Knowledge-limit disclaimers and guesses

- «وفقا للمعلومات المتاحة»
- «لا تتوفر تفاصيل كثيرة»
- «يعتقد أنه»
- «من المرجح أنه»
- «جميع المعلومات غير متاحة»

State exactly what the cited material establishes and what remains unknown. Never fill a gap with a plausible guess.

### 24. A heading repeated in the first sentence

Remove the sentence that only restates the heading.

### 25. Writing about the document instead of its subject

- «يوضح الجدول التالي»
- «ينقسم هذا القسم إلى»
- «تم إعداد هذا المحتوى بالاعتماد على»
- «أي معلومة غير مؤكدة مذكورة كما هي»

Write about the subject. Keep source notes, methodology, and navigation instructions when the reader needs them.

## F. Writing for the wrong reader

Do not rebuild background the reader already has. In a reply, lead with the decision and keep only the reasoning that would change whether the reader agrees. Do not apply this rule to standalone documentation, incident reports, or knowledge-base articles.

## G. Technical and source integrity

Arabic versions of the placeholders and leaked markers listed in sections 27 to 31 of `SKILL.md` are common. Keep technical identifiers in their original form: product names, commands, paths, protocols, API names, and error messages stay in Latin script even when the surrounding text is Arabic.

## Arabic writing rules

- Keep natural Arabic syntax and correct agreement. Do not import English sentence order.
- Prefer a direct verb over a heavy nominalization when the meaning is the same.
- Keep the writer's register: a formal white paper and a chat message should not end up in the same voice.
- Keep the established English term beside its Arabic equivalent on first use for technical topics, then use one of the two consistently.
- Do not translate product names, commands, paths, environment variables, registry keys, or API names.
- Keep the diacritics the writer uses. Do not add diacritics that were not there, and do not remove them from a religious text, a quotation, or a name.
- Keep mixed-language punctuation as the writer used it: the comma, semicolon, and question mark stay in the position the writer placed them.
